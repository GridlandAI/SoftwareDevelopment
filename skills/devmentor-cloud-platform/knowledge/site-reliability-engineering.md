# Site Reliability Engineering

**Domain:** 09 - Cloud Infrastructure and Platform
**Primary sources:** The Art of Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) with Azure: Building and Deploying Applications That Endure, 2nd Edition (Huete Beloki, 2025)

## Overview

Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) is the discipline of applying software-engineering practices to operations so that systems are reliable at scale. Originating at Google, it reframes "operations" as an engineering problem: instead of accepting manual, reactive firefighting, SRE teams measure reliability objectively, automate away repetitive work, and make data-driven trade-offs between shipping features and protecting stability. The central insight is that 100% reliability is the wrong target. It is hard to achieve, insanely expensive to maintain, and usually invisible to users, so reliability is set as an explicit, negotiated objective and everything else (release velocity, on-call load, investment) is managed against it (Huete Beloki).

SRE is closely related to DevOps but sharper. DevOps focuses on delivering value and breaking down silos; SRE focuses specifically on reliability and gives it measurable instruments: service-level indicators, objectives, and agreements (SLI/SLO/SLA); error budgets that gate change; and metrics like MTTF, MTTR, and MTBF. SRE teams typically balance their time roughly 50% on engineering and 50% on operations, with a hard cap on operational "toil." This file captures the vendor-neutral SRE principles alongside their Azure realizations (Azure Monitor, Log Analytics, Application Insights, Service Health, Chaos Studio, Load Testing, Logic Apps for incident automation). The principles transfer to any platform; Azure is the concrete toolchain.

## Core concepts

- **Reliability.** The acceptable uptime/behavior of a system from the user's point of view. It has many facets beyond raw availability: latency (delay between request and response), throughput, freshness (recency of data), correctness, quality (degraded-but-working responses), and coverage (how much expected input was actually processed). (Huete Beloki)
- **Risk assessment.** SRE is continuous risk management: deciding when to optimize stability versus ship features, and agreeing an acceptable level of unreliability with the business. If the business agrees on 99.9% availability, aiming for more is usually wasted effort. (Huete Beloki)
- **Availability over time and over aggregate.** A 99.9% target allows ~1.44 min/day, ~43.8 min/month, or ~525 min/year of downtime, but availability is not linear and should be measured across the aggregate of redundant instances and regions, focusing on the criticality of the affected component (failing checkout is worse than failing password reset). Never run a single instance. (Huete Beloki)
- **RTO and RPO.** Two non-functional requirements set during risk assessment: Recovery Time Objective (maximum acceptable downtime after an incident) and Recovery Point Objective (maximum acceptable data loss). They drive HA/DR architecture and SLA feasibility. (Huete Beloki)
- **Toil.** Not all manual work, but specifically work that is manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive, devoid of lasting value, and that scales linearly with the service. The SRE goal is to eliminate or reduce toil so engineers spend time on durable improvements. (Huete Beloki)
- **Three pillars of observability.** Logs (timestamped event descriptions, often needing structure/parsing), metrics (point-in-time numeric measurements for alerts and reporting), and traces (how a single request flows through components in a distributed system). Observability lets you ask new questions about internal state from external outputs; monitoring is the actionable process of collecting data and deciding on it. (Huete Beloki)
- **Westrum culture.** Organizational culture (pathological/power-oriented, bureaucratic/rule-oriented, generative/performance-oriented) strongly affects information flow. Generative cultures train messengers, treat failure as inquiry, share risk, and encourage bridging, which is the foundation for blameless postmortems and psychological safety. DORA research links this culture to performance. (Huete Beloki)

## Frameworks & models

- **SLI / SLO / SLA.** The measurement backbone of SRE. The **SLI** is the indicator: a customer-centric metric that measures one reliability aspect (for example, the percentage of API requests returning 2xx within 200 ms). It must be measured from the user's vantage point, not the server's. The **SLO** is the goal set on that indicator based on user expectation (for example, 99.5% of those requests successful over 24 hours); it is the lowest acceptable level before users complain and is the driver for what SRE teams work on. The **SLA** is the contractual agreement with the customer, usually looser than the internal SLO to leave margin, and carries financial penalties if breached. An SLA is the least technical of the three and reflects compensation thresholds, not actual uptime. (Huete Beloki)
- **Error budget and burn rate.** The error budget is the allowed margin of unreliability: effective availability minus the SLO, or simply `1 − SLO`. A 99.9% monthly SLO grants ~43.2 minutes of budget. While budget remains, teams may ship changes freely; when it is exhausted, change velocity should slow or freeze until stability is restored. **Burn rate** measures how fast the budget is consumed (a burn rate of 1 over the SLO period consumes the whole budget exactly by period end); alerts are defined on burn rate evaluated over both a short and a long window to catch fast burns without noise. (Huete Beloki)
- **MTTF / MTTR / MTBF (and MTTD).** Incident-management metrics. **MTTF** (mean time to failure) measures average time until an unrepairable component fails (used for hardware life span and platform quality, not team quality). **MTTR** (mean time to repair/restore) measures average time to troubleshoot and recover, reflecting team and tooling quality; sub-phases include mean time to detect (MTTD), notify, and acknowledge. **MTBF** (mean time between failures) measures average time between repairable outages. Higher SLAs demand faster MTTR and rarer failures. (Huete Beloki)
- **Composite SLA.** Combining service components multiplies their SLAs (an App Service at 99.95% behind a database lowers the combined number); multi-region active-active deployment with a geo load balancer raises it. The arithmetic shows three regions is typically the sweet spot beyond which extra regions barely improve the SLA. (Huete Beloki)
- **Dickerson's hierarchy of reliability.** A Maslow-style pyramid of SRE priorities from foundational to advanced: Monitoring → Incident Response → Blameless Postmortems → Test/Release processes → Capacity Planning → Development → UX/Product. You cannot meaningfully work on a higher layer until the layers beneath are solid (you cannot do good incident response without monitoring, etc.). (Huete Beloki)
- **Incident Command System (ICS).** Google's incident-response process derives from the firefighting ICS (1968). Its basic principles are a clear chain of command, clearly defined roles and responsibilities, disciplined documentation, and resolving the incident as fast as possible. (Huete Beloki)

## Patterns

- **Error-budget gating** - Intent: use the remaining error budget as the objective control on change velocity; ship freely while budget remains, freeze risky changes when it is exhausted. When to use: any team balancing feature delivery against a reliability SLO. Trade-offs: aligns dev and ops incentives objectively vs. requiring trustworthy monitoring and an agreed SLO. (Huete Beloki)
- **Multi-window burn-rate alerting** - Intent: alert on how fast the error budget is burning, evaluated over both a long window (e.g., 1 hour) and a short window (e.g., 5 minutes), to catch fast burns early while suppressing noise. When to use: SLO-based alerting in production. Trade-offs: timely, low-noise alerts vs. more configuration than simple threshold alerts. (Huete Beloki)
- **Toil reduction through automation** - Intent: detect recurring incidents with alert rules and auto-remediate with runbooks (restart, scale, reconfigure), notifying an engineer only to confirm the fix. When to use: repetitive, automatable, reactive issues that wake people at 2 a.m. Trade-offs: frees engineering time and improves morale/retention vs. up-front automation investment. In Azure: Azure Monitor alert rules + Azure Automation runbooks. (Huete Beloki)
- **Sustainable on-call rotation** - Intent: provide coverage to critical services without harming engineers, using follow-the-sun shifts, primary/secondary escalation, "everyone takes the pager" (including developers when the app layer is involved), flexible schedules set weeks ahead, and a cap on ops load. When to use: any team owning production services. Trade-offs: reliable coverage and shared ownership vs. scheduling effort; Google caps ops activities at 50% of SRE time. (Huete Beloki)
- **Defined incident roles** - Intent: bring order to chaos by assigning an incident commander (drives resolution, coordinates "who does what," usually non-technical), first/second responders (on-call engineers), a scribe (documents the timeline), a communications lead (the public face, updates customers and stakeholders), and technical leads/SMEs. When to use: any non-trivial incident. Trade-offs: clarity and parallelism vs. requiring trained, rehearsed roles. (Huete Beloki)
- **Blameless postmortem** - Intent: treat every incident as a learning opportunity; build a documented timeline (alerts, on-call engineers, tracking history, discussions) and focus on the system, never the person, assuming everyone acted with the best intentions given what they knew. When to use: after every significant incident. Trade-offs: surfaces real systemic causes and builds psychological safety vs. requiring a genuine cultural shift away from finger-pointing (the hardest but highest-impact change). (Huete Beloki)
- **Chaos engineering / reliability testing** - Intent: inject failures and load into systems (Azure Chaos Studio, Azure Load Testing) to validate resilience under real conditions before incidents occur, shifting effort from reactive to proactive. When to use: mature systems pursuing proven fault tolerance. Trade-offs: strongest resilience evidence vs. needing controlled blast radius. (Huete Beloki)

## Principles & best practices

- **Aim for the right reliability, not 100%.** Set an explicit SLO with the business; over-engineering reliability users cannot perceive wastes money better spent on features. (Huete Beloki)
- **Measure SLIs from the customer's point of view.** A server reporting 100% uptime can still feel broken to users on a VPN at 9 a.m. Observability across every layer is required to measure the right indicators. (Huete Beloki)
- **Make monitoring the foundation.** Per Dickerson's hierarchy, monitoring underpins everything; you cannot improve what you cannot measure, and SLI/SLO definitions depend on it. (Huete Beloki)
- **Integrate developers and operations into the SRE team.** Much of what threatens reliability (patches, releases, features) sits outside a pure ops team; sharing the pager motivates developers to improve monitoring and reduce toil. (Huete Beloki)
- **Document during incidents.** Recording debugging, mitigation, and collaboration activity in real time is the raw material for robust postmortems and continuous improvement. (Huete Beloki)
- **Maximize proactive over reactive work.** Use smart detection, availability tests, and chaos experiments to prevent incidents rather than only responding to them. (Huete Beloki)
- **Use OKRs tracking service, customer satisfaction, and employee morale.** Reliability metrics alone are insufficient; align teams with objective and key results across all three. (Huete Beloki)
- **Capacity planning and resilient architecture.** Invest in scalable, redundant, multi-region designs so the system absorbs high load and regional unavailability without breaching SLOs. (Huete Beloki)

## Anti-patterns & pitfalls

- **Chasing 100% reliability.** Disproportionately expensive and usually imperceptible to users; the wrong goal. (Huete Beloki)
- **Confusing SLA with actual uptime.** An SLA is a compensation threshold, not a measured availability figure; treating it as the operating target misframes the work. (Huete Beloki)
- **Running a single instance.** Immediately excludes any acceptable availability; redundancy across instances, zones, and regions is mandatory. (Huete Beloki)
- **Blame culture.** Punishing messengers increases mean time to acknowledge and resolve because engineers go silent; it is harmful to the business and the opposite of a generative (Westrum) culture. (Huete Beloki)
- **Meaningless alerts and unmanaged toil.** Waking people repeatedly for the same automatable issue destroys morale and retention; automate it away. (Huete Beloki)
- **Only the ops team takes the pager.** Excludes the developers best placed to fix and prevent application-layer failures. (Huete Beloki)

## Decision guidance

- **Setting SLOs.** Derive the SLO from user expectation and business tolerance, set the SLA looser than the SLO to leave operating margin, and define SLIs that are customer-centric and measurable end to end. Re-evaluate as the product and usage change. (Huete Beloki)
- **Deciding whether to ship.** Consult the error budget. Plenty of budget left → ship and experiment. Budget nearly exhausted → freeze customer-facing changes and prioritize stability and reliability work. (Huete Beloki)
- **Designing alerts.** Prefer burn-rate alerts over static thresholds, evaluate over short and long windows, and auto-remediate recurring known issues with runbooks so humans are paged only for novel problems. (Huete Beloki)
- **Prioritizing reliability work.** Follow Dickerson's hierarchy bottom-up: fix monitoring before incident response, incident response before postmortems, and so on, since each layer depends on the ones below. (Huete Beloki)
- **Choosing region count.** Use composite-SLA arithmetic: two-to-three regions usually deliver the needed availability; more regions add cost and complexity for negligible SLA gain. (Huete Beloki)
- **Realizing SRE on Azure.** Monitor with Azure Monitor (metrics free by default), Log Analytics (logs/KQL), and Application Insights (traces, availability tests, smart detection); check platform status with Azure Service Health; track SLI/SLO/error budget in App Insights; automate incident response with Azure Alerts → Logic Apps (Teams channel + adaptive card + GitHub Issue) and Power BI for MTTR/MTBF reporting; test resilience with Chaos Studio and Load Testing. (Huete Beloki)

## Recent developments (2024–2026)

- "AI SRE" went mainstream: AI agents now ingest telemetry to triage alerts, perform automated root-cause analysis, and trigger self-healing remediation, with a growing market of AI SRE/incident tools through 2025–2026. (Augment Code, 2026, https://www.augmentcode.com/guides/ai-sre-ai-powered-site-reliability-engineering; IBM, 2026, https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/observability-trends)
- OpenTelemetry consolidated as the de facto, vendor-neutral instrumentation standard, and its semantic conventions became a prerequisite for quality AI-assisted anomaly detection; the Grafana 2025 Observability Survey documents OTel's central role. (Grafana Labs, 2025, https://grafana.com/observability-survey/2025/)
- Agentic AIOps reframed observability from dashboards toward outcomes: platforms continuously correlate logs, metrics, traces, and deploy events to cut alert noise and detect anomalies before failure, addressing widespread alert fatigue. (IBM, 2026, https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/observability-trends)
- SRE increasingly operates as part of platform engineering, with a "data layer" (Prometheus, OpenTelemetry, FluentBit) feeding an "intelligence layer" for automated detection, triage, and remediation inside internal developer platforms. (Augment Code, 2026, https://www.augmentcode.com/guides/ai-sre-ai-powered-site-reliability-engineering)
- Reliability practice extended to AI systems themselves: SLOs, error budgets, and observability are being applied to LLM inference latency, cost, and quality, as Kubernetes becomes the inference substrate per the CNCF 2025 survey.
