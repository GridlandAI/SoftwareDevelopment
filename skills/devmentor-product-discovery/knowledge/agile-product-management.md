# Agile Product Management and MVPs

**Domain:** 03 - Product Discovery and Planning
**Primary sources:** Agile Product Management (Box set): Minimum Viable Product with Scrum & Agile (Paul VII, 2017, Pashun Consulting)

## Overview
This material covers the practical mechanics of agile product management with Scrum as the delivery framework, oriented toward shipping a Minimum Viable Product (MVP). The central thesis is that an MVP is a learning instrument, not a feature-stripped product: its purpose is to validate assumptions about customers and the market with the least time and money, so future product, business, and marketing decisions rest on real data rather than guesses.

It is opinionated about the MVP being for early adopters, not the mass market, and warns repeatedly against both over-building (cramming features) and under-building (shipping something so thin it inspires no one). The Scrum scaffolding (roles, events, artifacts) provides the cadence; the MVP tips provide the product judgment about what to put in each increment.

## Core concepts

- **Minimum Viable Product (MVP).** The smallest product increment that lets you learn the most from real users. Distinct from a Minimum Marketable Product (MMP): the MVP optimizes for learning and risk reduction, not for the tiniest sellable feature set.
- **Early adopters.** The target audience for an MVP. They have a known, painful problem they are actively trying to solve, have already tried an inadequate workaround, and can pay. They buy your potential, not your finished product.
- **Validating vs invalidating MVPs.** A *validating MVP* is an inferior version of the final product (success confirms the hypothesis; failure is inconclusive). An *invalidating MVP* is a superior, often hand-crafted version (e.g., the concierge model); if customers will not pay even for the dream version, there is no point building the planned product. Start with the invalidating MVP to kill bad assumptions cheaply.
- **Customer story.** A short description of how a customer uses the product and gains value through a specific process. Value comes from a story (features used in sequence), not from features in isolation. Start an MVP from one prioritized customer story and work backward to the features needed.
- **Scrum empiricism.** Transparency, inspection, adaptation - the three pillars. Work is not "done" until the team says so by the definition of Done.

## Frameworks & models

**The Scrum framework (as used for MVP delivery).**
- **Three roles.** Product Owner (orders and clarifies the product backlog, owns value, accountable even when the team helps), Scrum Master (coaches, facilitates events, removes impediments, shields the team), Development Team (cross-functional, self-organizing, three to nine people, no titles or sub-teams, decides how much to take into a sprint).
- **Sprints.** Time-boxed iterations of one calendar month or less producing a potentially shippable increment.
- **Events / ceremonies.** Sprint Planning (max 8 hours for a one-month sprint - the "what" and "how"), Daily Scrum (15 minutes - yesterday/today/impediments), Sprint Review (4 hours - demo the increment to stakeholders, gather feedback, PO judges "done"), Sprint Retrospective (3 hours - inspect the *process*, not the product).
- **Artifacts.** Product Backlog (prioritized user requirements, owned by PO), Sprint Backlog (selected items plus the plan, owned by the team), Product Increment (the working, "done" output).
- **Scrum skeleton.** Product backlog → sprint backlog → sprint (with daily scrums) → potentially shippable increment → review/release → retrospective → repeat. "A simple, powerful mini-factory manufacturing shippable features every sprint."

**The MVP process (five sections from the box set).**
1. **Preparing the MVP.** Find a niche (study demographics, behaviors, segments; build a target-customer profile). Create a realistic, graphical roadmap of incremental releases by priority (made of epics/themes, not a contract). Conduct competitive research (focus on the top 20% who hold 80% of profits; analyze strengths, prices, positioning). Presell the MVP (validate willingness to pay before building; under-promise timelines and add a buffer). Test assumptions (customer interviews, landing pages, explainer videos, crowdfunding). Make sure you solve the right problem (validating vs invalidating MVPs). Focus on core functionalities (build a backlog, eliminate non-critical items, separate want vs need, defer features that need network scale).
2. **Developing the MVP.** Understand the product's core purpose (a central, authentic "why" that guides decisions and motivates the team). Do one thing exceptionally (learn to say no; build a list of things you will *not* do). Time-frame the development work (time-boxing forces focus, curbs perfectionism and multitasking, prevents over-engineering and budget overruns). Solve important problems (underserved, valued, monetizable). Build out more than the basics (a credible, complete-feeling MVP beats a thin one that inspires no one). Focus on customer experience (experience converts, not features; strip to the essential task and observe real behavior).
3. **Marketing the MVP.** Leave customers wanting more (Spark → Activity → Variable Reward → Commitment, the hook loop). Build a community via market segmentation (hobbies, age, life stories, demographics, geography). Base the MVP on customer stories.
4. **Testing the MVP.** Explainer video (script-first, short, benefit-led, measurable funnel). Landing page as MVP (single call-to-action, benefit layout, ad traffic, analytics, surveys). Alternative MVPs: Manual MVP (a façade you operate by hand), Concierge MVP (manual service mimicking the product), Single-Featured MVP (Dropbox/Google style - one feature done well).
5. **Other considerations.** Manage resources and contingency; leverage cloud services to keep fixed costs low.

## Patterns
- **Concierge MVP** - intent: validate value by manually delivering a premium version of the service. When to use: high market risk, when you can serve a few customers by hand. Trade-offs: not scalable; only proves demand, not the build.
- **Landing-page MVP** - intent: measure demand and capture intent before any build. When to use: testing willingness to sign up or pre-purchase. Trade-offs: measures interest, not sustained usage or willingness to pay over time.
- **Single-featured MVP** - intent: solve one important problem exceptionally rather than many partially. When to use: a labor-intensive problem that can be scaled down to a partial-but-complete solution. Trade-offs: risks looking too narrow; relies on picking the right one feature.
- **Cosmetic / "coming soon" link** - intent: measure desirability of a feature by counting clicks on a stub. When to use: deciding whether to build a speculative feature. Trade-offs: use with caution; can frustrate users if overused.
- **Hook loop (Spark → Activity → Variable Reward → Commitment)** - intent: drive repeat engagement. When to use: consumer products needing retention. Trade-offs: ethical concerns around addictive design; commitment step must genuinely improve the user's experience.

## Principles & best practices
- The MVP's job is to turn questions into answers; the more you build without releasing, the longer you delay learning.
- Build feedback pipelines early - they yield data and buy patience from customers when features are missing.
- A critical agile premise: time and money determine scope, not the other way around. The roadmap is a game plan, not a contractual guarantee.
- Three common arguments against a focused MVP, all to be resisted: "we need more value (features)," "we must address all customer desires," "we must complete it before launch." Features do not equal experience.
- Keep development teams durable and small; cross-functional, self-organizing, no titles.
- Use retrospectives to improve the process; use reviews to inspect the product.

## Anti-patterns & pitfalls
- **Feature cramming.** Too many features create an unstable MVP, poor experience, higher cost, and harder hypothesis testing.
- **Solving the wrong problem.** The most common cause of failure; working long on a problem nobody pays to solve. Invalidating MVPs exist to prevent this.
- **Treating the MVP as a thin throwaway.** Customers (and early adopters) are not loyal to mediocre products; an uninspiring MVP gains no momentum.
- **Roadmap as a promise.** Communicate that it will be refined; protect time and budget consistency.
- **Skipping willingness-to-pay validation.** Surveys and verbal interest are not the same as money committed.

## Decision guidance
- Choose invalidating MVP first when market risk is high (will anyone pay at all?), then a validating MVP to test a specific go-to-market method.
- Pick the MVP format by what you need to learn and how cheaply: landing page or explainer video for demand, concierge/manual for value of the experience, single-feature for proving a core job.
- Prioritize the backlog by repeatedly asking want vs need, who the early customer is, and whether a feature has value before a critical mass of users (defer social/search/profile features until you have scale).
- Size the development team three to nine; below that, delivery suffers; above it, coordination breaks down.
- Use Scrum's cadence (sprints, daily scrum, review, retro) as the delivery engine and the MVP tips as the product-judgment layer on top.

## Recent developments (2024–2026)

- **The "augmented Product Owner" and AI-assisted Scrum.** Scrum.org and others published 2024-2025 guidance on AI amplifying (not replacing) PO and Scrum Master roles: AI drafts and refines backlog items and acceptance criteria, clusters user feedback, summarizes reviews and retros, and keeps reporting current. A 2024 case study cited 86% of Scrum Masters saving 30 minutes to 2 hours per sprint on reporting, with reported gains in velocity and risk reduction. The consistent message is augmentation: human judgment on value, prioritization, and stakeholders remains the role's core. (Scrum.org, "The Augmented Product Owner" and "AI in Agile Product Teams," 2024-2025; European Scrum, "AI Agile Guide 2025")

- **Generative AI accelerates the MVP build itself.** AI now generates interactive mockups and working prototypes quickly, letting teams test concepts with users earlier and iterate, which directly serves Scrum's "early and continuous delivery." AI is also used to help identify the right minimum feature set by analyzing user needs and market signals, shifting effort from building the increment toward deciding what increment is worth building. (Miro, "MVP in Agile," 2025; Hyperdrive Agile, 2025)

- **MVP team-size guidance updated for AI roles.** 2025 team-structure writeups keep the small cross-functional team (roughly 3-5 for an early MVP, 5-9 at scale-up) consistent with the box set's "three to nine," but now fold in AI-adjacent specialists (MLOps, data/QA) as products incorporate AI features. (ThirstySprout, "Roles in Agile Software Development: The 2025 Guide"; Sombra, 2025)

- **Roles persist but must adapt.** The 2025 consensus (Devoteam, Scale-up Consultants) is that POs and Scrum Masters who adopt AI tooling stay relevant while those who do not risk redundancy, with AI handling manual tracking so practitioners focus on coaching, facilitation, and complex judgment. This reinforces the box set's emphasis on durable, small, self-organizing teams rather than changing the framework. (Devoteam, 2025; Scale-up Consultants, 2025)

- **Core mechanics stable.** Scrum's roles, events, and artifacts and the MVP-as-learning-instrument thesis (validating vs. invalidating MVPs, early adopters, customer stories, concierge/landing-page/single-feature tests) are unchanged. The 2024-2026 developments are AI tooling layered onto each ceremony and a faster, cheaper prototyping loop, not a new delivery framework.
