# PM Career and Frameworks

**Domain:** 03 - Product Discovery and Planning
**Primary sources:** Cracking the PM Career: The Skills, Frameworks, and Practices to Become a Great Product Manager (Jackie Bavaro & Gayle Laakmann McDowell, 2021, CareerCup)

## Overview
Cracking the PM Career is a comprehensive manual for becoming a great product manager and product leader. Its definition of the role is sharp: "A PM is the person on a product team who is responsible for choosing the right problems to go after, defining what success looks like, and guiding their team to achieve successful outcomes." The role is about *outcomes, not output*, and is fundamentally a "whitespace" role - the PM owns anything not covered by others - exercised through influence rather than authority.

The book organizes the craft into five skill areas (Product, Execution, Strategic, Leadership, People Management) and frames career progression not as a skills rubric but as growth in **scope, autonomy, and impact** - you earn promotion by demonstrating autonomy and impact at your current scope and building trust to operate at a larger one.

## Core concepts

- **The product triad.** Engineer (technical solution), Designer (user-experience solution), Product Manager (which problems to solve, what success looks like, how to get there). The best teams have all three involved from the start, sharing context and deferring to whoever owns a given decision.
- **The product life cycle (Discover → Define → Build → Launch).** *Discover*: figure out what problem is worth solving (feature requests, funnel metrics, interviews, concept tests, competitor/market analysis, design sprints). *Define*: narrow to a specific, feasible slice and frame it so the team is aligned on scope and success. *Build* and *Launch* follow. PMs usually run two streams at once - one nearer discovery, one nearer delivery - so engineers never wait.
- **Outcomes over output.** Being a great PM means reliably shipping *successful* products, not just following steps or shipping features that nobody uses.
- **Influence without authority.** PMs rarely have management authority over the engineers building the product; they lead with vision, research, data, design principles, and relationships.
- **Scope, autonomy, impact.** The real drivers of PM level and promotion, not a per-skill checklist.

## Frameworks & models

**Product strategy = Vision + Strategic Framework + Roadmap.** A complete product strategy has three parts that work together: the **vision** inspires (where we're heading and why it matters), the **strategic framework** explains the details (the spec for the strategy: high-level framing, goals, principles, target market, the bets and why), and the **roadmap** shows what it will take (prioritized, sequenced, roughly-costed plan with milestones). Smaller strategies should ladder up to broader ones.

**Vision creation.** Brainstorm an ambitious vision by removing an assumed-fixed constraint: "If we could wave a magic wand, what would the ideal experience be?" / "If we started the company from scratch today, what would ideal look like?" Work backward from the ideal to a roadmap, then decide the first step. Make the vision tangible (Airbnb's 100% Instant Book; the "minefield" image of everything that could go wrong, with the roadmap defusing each mine). An ambitious target lets you deprioritize work that the future would make obsolete.

**Creating a large strategy (Alex Hood / Asana 7-step process).** (1) Dive into lots of input (existing higher-level strategy, customer insights, tests, competitive analysis). (2) Kick it off (right stakeholders, DACI roles, frame as enhancing prior art). (3) Checkpoint 1 - key hypotheses (split into workstreams with owners; identify the small set of questions, two to seven, that validate/invalidate them). (4) Checkpoint 2 - draft strategies/bets (write strong, controversial assertions; co-create; winnow by voting, but it is not a democracy). (5) Checkpoint 3 - declare (finalize wording; determine the "so what" - what the team will do differently). (6) Create artifacts (communicate up, down, across; strong visuals; repeat often). (7) Create a scoreboard (broadcast how you keep score). Quick approaches for small/uncontroversial areas: Draft and Feedback, Half/Full-Day Strategy Retreat, or 30% Time over one to three weeks.

**Roadmapping and prioritization.** No single objectively correct prioritization method - it is about trade-offs and judgment. A weighted factor table can help but should not be blindly trusted. Benefit factors: Kano model (dissatisfiers/satisfiers/delighters - ship table-stakes first), size of user benefit, number of users, type of users, completeness of use case, business benefit, internal benefit, progress toward vision, risk/certainty. Cost factors: size of work, team capacity/skills, within-team dependencies, costing risk, other risks (security/scale/performance/brand). Avoid "snacking" only on low-cost/low-benefit work.

**Roadmap format.** Use uncertainty-aware time buckets ("now, next, later" or "this quarter / six months / a year"); write items as *problems to solve*, not fixed solutions; group by theme/initiative/goal; rough-cost with experienced engineers; align to strategy; revisit every three to six months and on major news; design for the audience (hide detail if salespeople would pre-sell unconfirmed features).

**DACI** (referenced for decision roles): Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed - used to clarify who decides on a strategy or decision.

## Patterns
- **Product triad partnership** - intent: build products with engineering, design, and PM jointly owning their domains from the start. When to use: any product team. Trade-offs: the split varies by seniority; mismatched expectations need an explicit up-front conversation.
- **Discover/Define before Build** - intent: avoid building the wrong thing by understanding the problem before committing to a solution. When to use: before any significant build. Trade-offs: takes time engineering may resist; mitigated by running discovery and delivery streams in parallel.
- **Vision → Framework → Roadmap** - intent: align people on where, why, and how with three complementary artifacts. When to use: any scope spanning multiple launches. Trade-offs: requires real strategic time, which PMs must defend against execution pressure.
- **Work backward from the ideal** - intent: find higher-impact directions by removing an assumed constraint and reasoning back to the first step. When to use: when incremental work is hitting diminishing returns (Airbnb Instant Book). Trade-offs: ambitious visions need evangelism to stick.
- **Now / Next / Later roadmap** - intent: communicate plans honestly without false date commitments. When to use: sharing roadmaps with uncertainty. Trade-offs: some audiences treat any roadmap as fixed - design and caveat accordingly.
- **Kano-based prioritization** - intent: sequence by emotional response (must-haves before delighters). When to use: feature prioritization. Trade-offs: classification is judgmental; combine with user/business benefit factors.

## Principles & best practices
- Strategy is more important than feature work; if the team lacks a good strategy, creating one is the top priority.
- Make the mindset shift from "great PM for my team" to "great PM for my whole company" to free up time for strategic work.
- Execution work expands to fill the time you give it; deliberately reserve time for strategic thinking, especially before a project ends and it is too late to change direction.
- Connect every project explicitly to the strategy; if a piece does not connect, do not plow ahead - reconnect it or revisit the strategy with your manager.
- Communicate the strategy until it sticks; PMs almost always underestimate how much repetition is needed (quiz the team to check).
- Do not overlook obvious, "boring" wins (default settings, onboarding bug fixes, funnel/monetization/email optimization, internationalization) - they often beat fancy features.
- Learn to say no; it is a core PM skill most people have not practiced.
- Progress as a PM by growing scope, autonomy, and impact and building trust - not by ticking a skills rubric.

## Anti-patterns & pitfalls
- **Taking an executive's solution literally.** Building "export to PDF" because a VP said so, skipping discovery, shipping a flop - and getting blamed for it.
- **Weak define phase.** Not aligning on problem scope and success, leading to a designer's six-month solution and "cutting anything means you don't care about customers."
- **Snacking on low-value work.** Filling the roadmap with cheap, low-impact items instead of tackling enough important work.
- **Treating the roadmap as set in stone.** Locking into dates/features/solutions and losing the ability to adapt.
- **Blind reliance on prioritization scores.** Refining a weighted formula until it gives the answer you wanted, missing the real trade-offs.
- **Under-communicating strategy.** Assuming people remember it after one telling.

## Decision guidance
- Define the PM role per team explicitly (especially the triad split and the First-90-Days expectation conversations) - the role differs by company and teammate seniority.
- Always run discovery before committing a solution; look for a problem large enough to be worth solving and feasible enough to succeed.
- Build a full strategy (vision, framework, roadmap) for any scope spanning multiple launches; pick the creation depth (quick draft vs full 7-step process) by stakes, team size, and controversy.
- Prioritize with a factor table for structure but apply judgment; ensure table-stakes are met (Kano) before investing in delighters, and check that small decisions add up to the vision.
- Communicate roadmaps as now/next/later, problems not solutions, grouped by theme, designed for the audience; revisit on a cadence and on major news.

## Recent developments (2024–2026)

- **AI is absorbing the "executor" half of the role.** The 2025-2026 consensus is that AI is taking over backlog grooming, ticket writing, sprint coordination, and requirement translation, pushing PMs toward the strategic and leadership skills this book centers (choosing the right problems, vision, discovery, influence). Lenny Rachitsky's position is representative: the role endures and evolves, and soft skills (influence, creativity, empathy) become more valuable, not less. This strengthens the book's "outcomes over output" and "strategy is the top priority" theses. (Lenny Rachitsky / "The AI-Native Product Manager," Maven, 2025; Userpilot, "Product Management in 2026")

- **Marty Cagan's read after two-plus years of AI PM.** Cagan argues AI mainly accelerates and improves how the existing craft is done (prototyping, discovery, evidence-gathering) rather than inventing a new operating model, and that empowered product teams and strong discovery matter more, not less. This is consistent with the book's product-triad and discover-before-build guidance. (airfocus by Lucid, "5 ways AI will impact product management - Marty Cagan's predictions," 2024-2025; Cagan, LinkedIn, 2025)

- **The generalist "PM in the middle" model is fragmenting.** Multiple 2026 analyses argue the single generalist role is splitting under the weight of distinct jobs, with AI-native and AI-literate PMs in demand and specialization (e.g., AI/ML product fluency, evals, data) increasingly expected. "Using AI ethically and wisely" is cited as more decisive for 2026 success than knowing how to code. This refines the book's five-skill-area framing toward AI literacy as a sixth competency. (Communications of the ACM, "Essential Skills for Next-Gen Product Managers"; eICTA/IIT-K, "Why AI Will Define Product Management," 2025-2026)

- **Hiring concentrating at the senior end.** 2025-2026 market reporting describes senior PM and PM-leadership hiring rising year over year, with companies betting on fewer, more experienced PMs covering a larger surface area, raising the importance of the scope/autonomy/impact progression model the book uses. (Eleken, "How to Become an AI Product Manager in 2026")

- **Strategy and roadmap frameworks stable.** Vision + strategic framework + roadmap, now/next/later roadmaps written as problems not solutions, Kano-based prioritization, DACI, and outcome-orientation remain the standard craft, now widely paired with OKRs and AI-assisted synthesis. The 2024-2026 change is task automation and AI literacy expectations layered on top, not a new model of the PM role. (Product School, "AI Product Managers...in 2026")
