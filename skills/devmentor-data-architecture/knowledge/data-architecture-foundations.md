# Data Architecture Foundations

**Domain:** 08 - Data Architecture and Engineering
**Primary sources:** Data Architecture: From Zen to Reality (Tupper, 2011); Data Architecture: The Information Paradigm, 2nd ed. (Inmon, 1992)

## Overview
Data architecture is the discipline of planning the structure, ownership, storage, and flow of an enterprise's data before any system is built, so that data behaves as a durable, reusable corporate asset rather than a byproduct of individual applications. It sits beside business, application, and technical architecture in the enterprise-architecture picture (the four TOGAF domains), but it has a special status: data outlives the processes that touch it. Processes change far more often than the entities they operate on, so an architecture organized around the inherent structure of data (customer, product, order) insulates the business from churn in a way that a process-first design never can.

This file captures the classic, foundational view of data architecture: what it is, how data is modeled across conceptual, logical, and physical layers, the rules of normalization and denormalization, master and reference data, metadata and governance, and the canonical "information paradigm" that separates operational systems of record from decision-support data. These ideas predate the cloud and the modern data stack, but they remain the substrate underneath data lakes, lakehouses, warehouses, and data mesh. Tupper supplies the modeling and warehouse craft; Inmon supplies the architectural worldview that birthed the data warehouse.

## Core concepts

**Architecture as a plan before construction.** Architecture is an analytical effort created prior to any construction activity: an abstracted framework that guides building from start to finish. The core skill is contextual analysis (defining the problem within the forces that bound it), not the construction itself. The recurring principle: analyze and define an area before doing any activity within it; short-term vision and premature scoping create artificial boundaries and rework. (Tupper)

**Form follows function, but design to the form.** A collection of data's inherent characteristics ("the form") makes it good at some functions and poor at others. Design structures from the inherent characteristics of the data and let functions be captured as they evolve, rather than contorting the form to fit one function. Well-designed forms are reusable across many functions. (Tupper)

**Data as a renewable, reusable asset.** The "modal shift" an enterprise must make is to perceive data as a leverageable asset, not a necessary cost of doing business. The data-driven competitor with the best data (speed, integrity, applicability) serves clients faster and responds to market forces with less impact. (Tupper)

**The information paradigm (Inmon).** The master mental model of classic data architecture: data divides into an **operational environment** (transaction-oriented, runs the day-to-day business at a detailed level) and a **decision-support (DSS) environment** (used to manage the business: reporting, trend analysis, ad hoc query, projections). The two are fundamentally different and must be physically separated. (Inmon)

**System of record.** Within the operational environment, the system of record is the repository where every piece of detailed, up-to-the-second operational data has a singular, nonredundant existence. It is the authoritative basis for reconciliation: all detailed occurrences of data outside it must reconcile back to it. Supporting operational data (transaction front ends like ATMs, distributed copies servicing a claim away from its domicile, high-probability archival data) is part of the operational environment but is not the system of record. (Inmon)

**The three DSS levels: atomic, departmental, individual.** The DSS environment has three layers. **Atomic data** (Inmon's original name for the data warehouse, also "foundation data," "information warehouse," "information factory") is the finest-grained DSS data, stored as of moments in time (time-variant), nonredundant, organized by major subject area, and fed from the system of record as access probability declines. **Departmental (functional) data** is summarized and derived from atomic data, peculiar to each department's needs, with high cross-department redundancy and regular scheduled reporting. **Individual data** is heuristic, almost entirely derived, autonomous end-user analysis built on departmental or atomic data. Three levels are optimal for most organizations but not magical. (Inmon)

**Primitive vs. derived data.** The most fundamental delineation. **Primitive** data describes a single entity or event (a customer's balance, an accident's details); it exists only while that entity exists, is detailed, accessed in small amounts very frequently, dynamic in content but stable in definition, used clerically to run the business. **Derived** data relates to multiple entities/events (an average balance, daily total sales); it is summarized, accessed infrequently in large amounts, static in content but dynamic in definition, used managerially to direct the business. Whether data is primitive or derived depends on the beholder (foreman vs. plant manager), which is why scope of integration must be defined carefully. A small amount of **transition data** is both at once (a bank's interest rates: derived from analysis, yet primitive when applied to a customer's account). The information paradigm mandates separating primitive from derived. (Inmon)

**The information cycle (data → information → knowledge → wisdom).** Primitive data supports operational processing and is "just data." Atomic data supports information. Departmental data is a basis of knowledge. Individual data is the foundation of wisdom. Data flows from operational → atomic → departmental → individual; individual-level processing influences the business, which operational systems then re-measure, closing the cycle. (Inmon)

**Public vs. private data.** Only public data (relevant to multiple functional units, tied to the mainstream business) must conform to the information paradigm. Private data (relevant to at most one unit and not the mainstream business, e.g. a utility's securities trading, an office worker's personal calendar) is exempt. The corporate data administrator adjudicates ambiguous cases. (Inmon)

## Frameworks & models

**Enterprise architecture frameworks (the four dominant).** (Tupper)
- **Zachman Framework** - a taxonomy. A 6×6 grid: six descriptive foci (data, function, network, people, time, motivation, i.e. what/how/where/who/when/why) by six player perspectives (planner, owner, designer, builder, subcontractor, enterprise). Rules: every artifact lives in exactly one cell; an architecture is complete only when every cell is sufficiently populated; cells in a column must trace to each other (an owner's business requirement must be traceable down to the database design, and vice versa).
- **TOGAF** - a process. Divides EA into business, application, data, and technical architecture. Its core is the Architecture Development Method (ADM), eight cyclic phases moving from generic to specific along the "enterprise continuum" (Foundation → Common Systems → Industry → Organizational). Complementary to Zachman: Zachman categorizes artifacts, TOGAF produces them. Highly adaptable (phases can be reordered, skipped, combined).
- **FEA (Federal Enterprise Architecture)** - the most complete: both a template and a process. Enterprise as segments (core-mission vs. business-services) plus enterprise services. Five reference models: business (BRM), components (CRM), technical (TRM), data (DRM), performance (PRM), all to establish a common business language and surface duplicative investment.
- **Gartner** - a practice, not a taxonomy or process. You engage Gartner for reputation and expertise rather than for a prescribed method.

**Data modeling notations.** Entity-Relationship (Chen, 1976), Information Engineering (IE, all relationships binary), IDEF1X (US Air Force standard, semantically weaker but learnable), and Object Role Modeling (ORM). Top-down, entity-first modeling (ER/IE) is more concise and usable than bottom-up data-element harvesting. (Tupper)

**The three model layers (conceptual → logical → physical).** (Tupper)
- **Conceptual data model (CDM)** - how the business sees its information. Highly abstracted, captures major entities and that they relate, without explicit cardinality, type, or domain constraints. Paired with a functional decomposition diagram.
- **Logical data model (LDM)** - a structured, technology-neutral interpretation: entities in normal form, many-to-many relationships resolved into associative entities, candidate and primary identifiers chosen, attributes attributed to their single best home, keys propagated as foreign keys, domains and constraint rules defined. Impartial to use; naturally associates data.
- **Physical model** - the LDM adapted to a target DBMS: tables, columns, indexes, primary/foreign keys implemented, denormalization applied for performance, null and special-restriction handling, accompanied by access-path diagrams that reveal performance-critical structures.

**Model repository and model-driven releases.** Conceptual/logical/physical models are kept in a versioned repository with check-in/check-out, bridging an architectural/planning tool → logical modeling tool → physical design tool. Changes flow from the enterprise architectural model through a staging model into the enterprise logical model, are carved out per application release, transformed into application database models (ADBM), forward-engineered to DDL, and reconciled back into the enterprise model. Supports "Release/release" methodology and release stacking (one release clears a test environment before the next enters). The data administrator (DA) owns logical/enterprise modeling; the DBA owns physical design. (Tupper)

**Inmon's conceptual foundation.** At the heart of the paradigm is a primitive conceptual (data or data-and-process) model. Only primitive information must be fully captured; derived data need not be modeled exhaustively because much is produced heuristically and unpredictably. A sufficiently detailed primitive foundation lets analysts derive freely. (Inmon)

## Patterns

**Subject-area orientation** - Intent: organize the warehouse/atomic layer around the major subjects of the enterprise (customer, product, claim) rather than by application or process. When to use: any integrated, nonvolatile decision-support store. Trade-offs: gathers physically scattered operational data into a coherent, reusable structure and insulates it from process change; requires up-front enterprise data analysis. (Inmon; Tupper)

**Star schema (dimensional model)** - Intent: a central fact table of numeric measures surrounded by denormalized dimension tables, so common analytical queries need few joins. When to use: the consumer-facing detail layer of a warehouse / data marts, where end users query directly and need understandability. Trade-offs: dramatically simpler queries (Kimball: ~80% become single-table browses) and fast reads; heavy denormalization, data duplication, loses information through aggregation, unstable when query requirements shift. Define the grain first. (Tupper)

**Normalized enterprise data warehouse, dimensional marts** - Intent: keep the foundational EDW normalized (third normal form) for consistency, integrity, and flexibility; derive dimensional star schemas into data marts/cubes for end-user access. When to use: large enterprises wanting both modular extensibility and query usability; the Inmon-style hub-and-spoke counter to a pure Kimball axiomatic approach. Trade-offs: structured, change-resilient, reflects the real structure of the data; more layers and ETL than building marts directly from business processes. (Tupper)

**Denormalization for performance** - Intent: deliberately reintroduce redundancy into a normalized model to cut joins and speed retrieval. When to use: read-heavy access where the normalized model's join cost dominates. Trade-offs: faster reads and fewer objects; introduces update/insert anomalies and consistency burden; must be justified and documented so it can be renormalized later. (Tupper)

**Generic domain-constraint / reference table** - Intent: house many limited-set validation values (code tables, valid ranges, translations, derivation algorithms) without propagating foreign keys into the constraint tables, optionally aggregating multiple constraint types behind a "type" discriminator. When to use: validation/lookup data shared by many entities. Trade-offs: avoids an enormous foreign-key web; constraint tables stay independent reference data. (Tupper)

**Time as a database dimension** - Intent: model history explicitly (activation/birth, state changes, termination) and archive data according to its structure at the time of archival. When to use: any system that must follow a trail of recorded activity or answer historical queries. Trade-offs: enables trend analysis and audit; archival/retrieval is hard when archived structure differs from current structure; raise the issue in every phase from architecture to access tuning. (Tupper)

**System of record + downstream reconciliation** - Intent: every detailed datum has one authoritative home; all copies reconcile back to it. When to use: any environment with distributed or replicated operational data. Trade-offs: eliminates conflicting values and provides a single basis for reconciliation; requires disciplined ownership and a clear boundary for what the system of record contains. (Inmon)

## Principles & best practices
- Plan before you build, and design with the user in mind; integrate the structure into its environment. (Tupper, after Christopher Alexander)
- Architecture leads to foundational stability, not rigidity; keep it a living construct that evolves with the business, or it ages and dies. (Tupper)
- Design without time as a boundary: extensible, flexible structures survive longer because change costs less; build structures only as big as they need to be. (Tupper)
- Define data ownership at the outset, element by element, including all CRUD authority; under normal circumstances one and only one operating department owns a given occurrence of data at a given moment. (Tupper data-policy principles; Inmon)
- Capture, validate, and scrub data at the earliest point in the process (at the golden source) so downstream processes inherit clean data. (Tupper)
- Foster data sharing as policy so business decisions are consistent across business areas. (Tupper)
- Separate primitive from derived data; separate operational from decision-support. Do not run analytics on the transaction store. (Inmon)
- Normalize for the model of record (consistency, integrity, share-ability, independence from use); denormalize only as a justified, documented performance decision. (Tupper)
- Keep the enterprise data model the strategic foundation: it delineates data by inherent structure, making it dependent on business objects but independent of business processes. (Tupper)

## Anti-patterns & pitfalls
- **Analysis paralysis / investigative architecture** - overemphasizing requirements collection, following every data and process path to conclusion, capturing out-of-scope and ill-defined requirements; the result is an architecture doomed to fail and packages mis-selected against bad requirements. (Tupper)
- **Massive rigid structures** - tightly bound, inflexible designs that corner cases and entropy destroy quickly; the "wall" parable: lighter, open structures absorb the pressures of time. (Tupper)
- **Pie-in-the-sky vs. unlearned architecture** - an architecture too ambiguous to use day-to-day, or one bypassed because management does not support it, degenerates into an alien, rigid mechanism. (Tupper)
- **Overnormalization** - breaking naturally grouped, intelligence-bearing items (a zip code that encodes a state, an address, a phone number) into components that lose meaning and force joins to reassemble. (Tupper)
- **Running DSS on OLTP stores** - operational tables are (de)normalized for transaction efficiency, have stripped redundancy useful for analysis, and require complex multi-table joins and inefficient large-volume retrieval; analytics there causes contention and poor performance. (Inmon; Tupper)
- **Redundant, unintegrated operational data** - multiple conflicting copies of the same datum (two account balances for one social security number) corrupt downstream processing; the lower an organization sits on the integration scale, the more it wastes resources and aggravates synchronization. (Inmon)
- **Data swamps / no governance** - collecting data without ownership, classification, or quality discipline turns a store into an unusable dump. (Tupper warehouse "bad concepts")

## Decision guidance
- **Modeling layer to use:** stay technology-neutral through conceptual and logical modeling; do not identify the target DBMS or make physical trade-offs until the physical-modeling phase, when you adapt for that environment's efficiency.
- **Normalize or denormalize:** default to third normal form for the model of record and the foundational warehouse (no bias to read or write, maximum flexibility and integrity). Denormalize only when a known read-heavy access pattern justifies it, and document the decision for future renormalization. Use dimensional/star schemas at the consumer-facing detail layer where end-user understandability and query speed dominate.
- **Operational vs. decision-support:** put up-to-the-second, nonredundant, primitive data in the system of record; feed time-variant, subject-oriented, nonvolatile primitive data into the atomic/warehouse layer as access probability declines; let departmental and individual layers derive from there. Never co-locate heuristic analytical workloads with transaction processing.
- **Inmon vs. Kimball at the warehouse:** for a large enterprise wanting a consistent, change-resilient hub, model the EDW normalized from an enterprise data model and spin dimensional marts off it (Inmon/Tupper structured approach); a pure axiomatic star-from-business-process approach (Kimball) is faster to a usable mart but risks incorrect design, information loss through aggregation, and instability as query needs shift.
- **EA framework choice:** use Zachman to classify and ensure completeness of artifacts, TOGAF/ADM to drive the creation process from generic to specific, FEA when you need both plus a common reference-model language across many units. Match framework formality to system complexity: a single-user system needs no architect; an enterprise-wide, mission-critical, distributed system needs database, solutions, infrastructure, business, and enterprise architects coordinated by the enterprise architect.
- **Ownership and governance first:** decide data ownership element-by-element before sharing; designate one owning department per occurrence; commit data policy at all management levels or the data-driven engine stalls.

## Recent developments (2024–2026)

- **Inmon and Kimball are treated as complementary layers, not rival camps.** Modern practice combines Data Vault for flexible historized integration of many sources, a normalized Inmon-style enterprise core, and a Kimball dimensional delivery layer, exactly the structured EDW-to-mart design this file recommends. The classic conceptual/logical/physical and normalize-for-record/denormalize-for-read principles remain the substrate. (Simon Spati, "Inmon vs Kimball," 2024, https://www.ssp.sh/brain/inmon-vs-kimball/; Luis Oliveira, "Kimball, Inmon, and Data Vault," 2025, https://medium.com/@lgsoliveira/exploring-data-warehouse-modeling-methodologies-kimball-inmon-and-data-vault-c46f0a253190)
- **Dimensional models became the stable base for the AI semantic layer.** As organizations expose data to AI agents, a well-modeled dimensional/star layer provides the structured, stable foundation a governed semantic layer (and tools reasoning over it) can rely on, reasserting "design to the form" and subject-area orientation in an AI context. (ml4devs, "Dimensional Data Modeling," 2025, https://www.ml4devs.com/what-is/dimensional-data-modeling/)
- **The system-of-record / reconciliation idea reappeared as data versioning and time travel.** Open table formats (notably Apache Iceberg) provide snapshot isolation and time-travel queries, letting teams query data "as of" any point, a technical realization of this file's "time as a database dimension" and authoritative-record principles. (Secoda, "Best Data Versioning Tools 2025," 2025, https://www.secoda.co/blog/best-data-versioning-tools-2025)
- **Governance and ownership returned to the foreground as AI prerequisites.** Industry consensus in 2025-2026 holds that clean, owned, well-modeled data with clear ownership and contracts is the precondition for trustworthy AI, echoing this file's "define data ownership at the outset" and anti-swamp principles. (Thoughtworks, "The state of data mesh in 2026," 2026, https://www.thoughtworks.com/insights/blog/data-strategy/the-state-of-data-mesh-in-2026-from-hype-to-hard-won-maturity)
- **The operational/decision-support separation (Inmon) blurred technically but held conceptually.** HTAP and zero-ETL mirroring (e.g., operational stores auto-synced into a lake) reduce physical copying, yet practitioners still separate read-optimized analytical models from write-optimized transactional ones (CQRS), preserving the paradigm even as the boundary moves. (Microsoft Fabric Blog, "GA of Cosmos DB in Microsoft Fabric," 2025, https://blog.fabric.microsoft.com/en-US/blog/announcing-ga-of-cosmos-db-in-microsoft-fabric-and-cosmos-db-mirroring/)
