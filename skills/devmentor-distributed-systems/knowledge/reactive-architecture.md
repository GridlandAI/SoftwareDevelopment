# Reactive Architecture

**Domain:** 07 - Distributed Systems, Scalability, and Resilience
**Primary sources:** Reactive Microservices Architecture: Design Principles for Distributed Systems (Boner, 2016)

## Overview
Modern systems are distributed from day one. Multi-core processors, cloud computing, mobile devices, and the Internet of Things mean that every nontrivial application must operate in the "wild ocean of non-determinism" that is a distributed system. Jonas Boner argues that the response to this reality is not to retreat to the monolith but to build on the Reactive principles, which are not new: they have been hardened for over forty years, going back to Carl Hewitt's Actor Model, Jim Gray and Pat Helland's work at Tandem, and Joe Armstrong's Erlang. Microservices, done well, are one of the most interesting recent applications of these principles, having learned from SOA's failures and successes and re-architected the good ideas using Reactive thinking and modern infrastructure.

A reactive microservice is defined by a small set of mutually reinforcing traits, with isolation as the foundation. Isolation is a prerequisite for resilience (the ability to heal from failure) and elasticity (the ability to scale and move), and it requires asynchronous communication boundaries that decouple services in time (allowing concurrency) and space (allowing distribution and mobility). Eliminating shared mutable state and minimizing coordination, contention, and coherency cost (as formalized in the Universal Scalability Law) is essential. This matters across the SDLC because the hard part of microservices is not the individual service, which is comparatively easy, but everything around them: discovery, coordination, security, replication, data consistency, failover, deployment, and integration.

## Core concepts

- **The Reactive Manifesto traits** - the foundation the book rests on. A reactive system is *responsive* (it responds in a timely manner), *resilient* (it stays responsive in the face of failure through replication, containment, isolation, and delegation), *elastic* (it stays responsive under varying load by scaling resources up and down), and *message-driven* (it relies on asynchronous message-passing to establish boundaries that provide loose coupling, isolation, and location transparency). Responsiveness is the goal; resilience and elasticity are the means; message-passing is the enabling mechanism.
- **Isolation** - the most important trait and the foundation for the others. It slices up the whole architecture, must be considered from day one, and even shapes team boundaries (Conway's Law). Failure isolation, containing and managing failure without it cascading through a workflow, is the bulkheading pattern.
- **Bounded context (DDD)** - each microservice models its own domain with its own ubiquitous language. Microservices are heavily influenced by Domain-Driven Design, which Boner names as the best place to start learning the surrounding concepts.
- **Eventual consistency and "exploit reality"** - reality is not consistent; there is no single absolute present. Information has latency (bounded by the speed of light), so everything we observe is from the past. Inside a microservice you live on a "safe island of determinism and strong consistency" (ACID); outside the boundary you enter non-determinism and must embrace eventual consistency. Pat Helland's "data on the inside" (the local present) versus "data on the outside" (events, the "blast from the past"; commands, the "hope for the future") frames this.
- **Location transparency** - decoupling in space: the ability at runtime to dynamically scale a service across cores or nodes without changing code. It requires the service to be addressable.
- **Addressability** - addresses must be *stable* (usable to refer to the service indefinitely regardless of where it is, whether running, stopped, suspended, upgrading, or crashed; a client can always send to the address, with messages possibly queued, resubmitted, delegated, logged, or dead-lettered) and *virtual* (one address can represent a whole set of runtime instances).
- **Back-pressure** - a mechanism in the communication protocol for agreeing on the velocity of data flow so a fast system cannot overload a slower one. Embodied in the Reactive Streams specification (Akka Streams, RxJava, Spark Streaming, Reactive-compatible drivers).
- **ACID 2.0** (Helland) - design principles for scalable, resilient APIs: *Associative* (grouping of messages does not matter, enabling batching), *Commutative* (ordering does not matter), *Idempotent* (duplication does not matter), and *Distributed*. Operations meeting these can converge without coordination.

## Frameworks & models

- **The traits of a reactive microservice** - Isolate all the things; Act autonomously; Do one thing, and do it well; Own your state, exclusively; Embrace asynchronous message-passing; Stay mobile, but addressable. Each builds on isolation.
- **Universal Scalability Law (Gunther)** - scalability is limited by contention (waiting on shared resources) and coherency cost (coordinating shared state). Minimizing both, via a share-nothing architecture and minimal coordination, is the path to scale, low latency, and high throughput.
- **Decoupling in time and space** - asynchronous boundaries decouple in time (concurrency) and space (distribution/mobility). Both are required to reach the compartmentalization needed for isolation and resilience.
- **Three waves of data** - "data at rest" (offline batch over HDFS), then the Lambda Architecture's hybrid speed-plus-batch layers (which add the complexity of two pipelines and a merge), then "data in motion" via pure stream processing, which is the model most interesting for microservices because streaming becomes both a communication protocol and a persistence solution (through event logging).
- **Coordination-avoidance** - minimize service-to-service coordination of state ("share silence") because strong consistency requires coordination, which caps scalability, throughput, latency, and availability. Tools include CRDTs (eventually consistent, conflict-free data structures that merge without coordination), causal consistency (the strongest consistency achievable in an always-available system, implemented with logical time), and the observation that many SQL operations can be performed without transactions.

## Patterns

- **Bulkheading (failure isolation)** - Intent: divide the system into isolated compartments so a failure in one cannot cascade and sink the whole, as watertight ship compartments contain a hull breach. When to use: any service boundary where you must contain and manage failure. Trade-offs: containment requires genuine isolation (the Titanic's bulkheads failed because they did not reach the ceiling, so water spilled over); achievable only by breaking the strong coupling of synchronous communication.
- **Service supervision / supervisor hierarchies** - Intent: manage failure orthogonally to the regular workflow by organizing services (or actors) in a hierarchy where a parent supervises and heals its subordinates. When to use: actor-style systems (Erlang, Akka) needing self-healing resilience. Trade-offs: requires asynchronous message-passing and a supervision-capable runtime; in exchange, failure handling is separated from business logic.
- **Asynchronous message-passing (the share-nothing default)** - Intent: communicate between services asynchronously and non-blocking (logic inside a service may be synchronous) to decouple in time and space. When to use: all inter-service communication. Trade-offs: makes network failure modes first-class instead of hiding them behind the leaky abstraction and fallacies of synchronous RPC, frees threads (a blocking call holds a scarce thread hostage), and turns ten sequential 100ms calls into one 100ms fan-out; the cost is harder reasoning and tooling. REST is a poor default for inter-service calls because it is almost always synchronous; use it sparingly and at the expense of decoupling, evolution, scale, and availability.
- **Location transparency via virtual addressing** - Intent: address a service through a stable, virtual address that hides whether it is local, remote, single, or replicated, so it can be scaled, relocated, and failed over without client changes. When to use: any elastic, mobile service. Trade-offs: requires runtime infrastructure (service discovery); enables load-balancing for stateless services, active-passive replication with sticky routing for stateful services, and relocation for locality of reference.
- **Service discovery (Inversion of Control)** - Intent: have each service report to the platform where it is running and how to reach it, instead of hardcoding addresses. When to use: any dynamic, elastic system (hardcoded addresses force a static deployment model that contradicts microservices). Trade-offs: needs a platform-provided discovery service; in return services stay decoupled and mobile.
- **API Gateway** - Intent: a single entry point that receives client requests, routes them to the right services (with protocol translation if needed), composes replies, and returns them, simplifying the client-to-service protocol and encapsulating internal structure. When to use: bridging external clients (which often assume synchronous communication) to an internal asynchronous system. Trade-offs: achieving high availability and scalability is hard with a centralized solution, so prefer a decentralized, platform-provided implementation; buy, do not build.
- **Publish-Subscribe backbone (modern ESB)** - Intent: logically decouple senders and receivers and route data by predefined rules using a scalable messaging system (Kafka, Kinesis) or an AP-style NoSQL store (Cassandra, Riak). When to use: once direct point-to-point communication grows beyond a handful of services and turns into an "incomprehensible mess of chatter." Trade-offs: reintroduces an ESB-like role, now as a scalable message queue/streaming backbone; for advanced routing, transformation, enrichment, splitting, or merging, use stream-processing tooling (Akka Streams, Apache Camel).
- **Back-pressured streaming (Reactive Streams)** - Intent: bridge systems with fully asynchronous, back-pressured real-time streams so a fast producer cannot overload a slow consumer. When to use: integration across systems and continuous, potentially unbounded streams. Trade-offs: improves interoperability, reliability, and performance of the composed system; requires Reactive Streams-compatible components on both ends.
- **Circuit Breaker (for integration)** - Intent: capture failures from a faulty (often external, untrusted) service, retry, and if failure persists quarantine the service for a period while it recovers. When to use: communicating with any external system you do not control, where you cannot trust happy-path behavior. Trade-offs: protects against cascading failure; production-grade implementations exist (Netflix Hystrix, Akka).
- **Saga** - Intent: manage long-running business transactions as a sequence of local transactional steps, each paired with a compensating reversing transaction, so the whole can be rolled back in reverse order if a step fails. When to use: cross-service consistency where distributed (two-phase) transactions are too slow, unscalable, and brittle. Trade-offs: a scalable, resilient alternative to distributed transactions, at the cost of designing compensations and tolerating intermediate inconsistency.

## Principles & best practices

- Make isolation a first-class, day-one concern; it dictates architecture and team structure.
- A service can only *promise* its own behavior by publishing its protocol/API; this autonomy means all information to resolve conflicts or repair under failure lives within the service, removing the need for coordination.
- "Micro" refers to scope of responsibility, not lines of code; apply the Unix philosophy and the Single Responsibility Principle (one reason to exist, one reason to change).
- Own your state exclusively; never push state into a shared database, which is "a disguised monolith." Expose only immutable facts derived from current state, never mutable state directly; let each service choose its own storage (RDBMS, NoSQL, time-series, or event log via Event Sourcing and CQRS).
- For non-blocking benefits, the whole request chain must participate, from dispatch through the service to the database and back.
- Assess client communication per use case; do not default to synchronous out of habit, and look for opportunities to weaken consistency/ordering guarantees (causality, read-your-writes) to find the minimal coordination that still gives intuitive semantics.
- Prefer logical time (vector clocks) over wall-clock time for coordination, to avoid clock skew across nodes.
- Buy core infrastructure (discovery, API gateway, supervision, circuit breakers) from the platform rather than building it yourself.

## Anti-patterns & pitfalls

- **The disguised monolith** - calling an architecture "stateless" while pushing state into a big shared database. This only delegates the problem to a third party, weakening data-integrity, scalability, and availability guarantees. A shared database is still a monolith.
- **Synchronous RPC as the default** - hiding network failure modes behind a leaky abstraction and the fallacies of distributed computing; holds scarce threads hostage and creates strong coupling and cascading-failure risk. REST as the default inter-service protocol falls into this trap.
- **Incomplete isolation** - bulkheads that do not reach the ceiling (the Titanic): partial isolation lets failures spill over and still take down the whole system.
- **Assuming a globally consistent "now"** - modeling distributed systems as if there were one absolute present and relying on distributed transactions to maintain it; upholding that illusion is extremely costly and makes systems slow, unscalable, and brittle.
- **Point-to-point sprawl** - letting every service talk directly to every other; beyond a handful of services this becomes an incomprehensible mess of chatter.
- **Trusting external systems** - assuming a system you do not control will follow the protocol or behave on the unhappy path; always guard integration with asynchrony, back-pressure, and circuit breakers.

## Decision guidance

- Start every reactive design from isolation; ensure each service is independently deployable, scalable, monitorable, and failable, with bounded contexts drawn from DDD.
- Default to asynchronous message-passing between services; reserve synchronous REST for the edge (external clients that assume it) and assess each client use case individually rather than globally choosing sync or async.
- Choose the weakest consistency that preserves intuitive business semantics: prefer coordination-free designs (CRDTs, ACID 2.0 operations) where possible, fall back to causal consistency when eventual consistency loses too much meaning, and use Sagas instead of distributed transactions for cross-service workflows.
- Add a publish-subscribe/streaming backbone once point-to-point communication stops scaling; use it as an integration bus and as a persistence mechanism (event logging) rather than as a monolith bridge.
- Make services location-transparent and addressable so the platform can scale, relocate, and fail them over; obtain discovery, gateways, supervision, and circuit breaking from the platform.
- Apply back-pressure on any flow where a fast producer could overwhelm a slow consumer, especially across system boundaries.

## Recent developments (2024–2026)
- **Virtual threads (Project Loom) weakened the core performance argument for reactive.** Java 21's virtual threads let the JVM run millions of cheap blocking threads, so the thread-starvation problem that motivated reactive (non-blocking) stacks no longer applies to many web workloads. The 2025 consensus is that reactive programming is now a more specialized choice (complex streaming, back-pressured pipelines) rather than a default. (profiletree.com, "Java Reactive Programming", 2025, https://profiletree.com/java-reactive-programming/)
- **Akka licensing change spawned Apache Pekko.** After Lightbend changed Akka's license (away from open source), the community forked the last Apache-licensed Akka into Apache Pekko, now the open-source actor/streams toolkit on the JVM; this is a material supply-chain consideration for teams adopting the actor model. (Akka.NET/Pekko ecosystem coverage, 2024-2025, https://akka.io/)
- **Akka itself repositioned as an "agentic AI platform."** Lightbend/Akka rebranded around building reliable, stateful AI agents, applying actor-model isolation, supervision, and message-passing to LLM-driven systems, an extension of the reactive traits to a new workload. (akka.io, 2025, https://akka.io/)
- **Back-pressure and Reactive Streams remain standard where streaming is genuinely needed.** For distributed stream processing with complex topologies, Akka/Pekko Streams and Reactive Streams stay the right tool; the shift is narrower scope, not abandonment. (profiletree.com, 2025)
- **The underlying principles are stable.** Isolation, message-driven boundaries, location transparency, sagas, circuit breakers, and coordination-avoidance (CRDTs, causal consistency, ACID 2.0) remain sound; reactive's place in the toolbox simply narrowed as the runtime landscape (virtual threads) changed.
